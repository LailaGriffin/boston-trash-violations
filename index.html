Nine in 10 of Boston’s trash-storage citations are at homes, and Dorchester’s total is more than double the neighborhood average

By Laila Griffin · Northeastern University, JRNL 3700

Boston has issued 33,225 citations for improper trash storage in 2026. Of those, 30,093 were at residential properties. Dorchester alone accounts for 6,494, about one of every five.

Those figures come from public data released by the City of Boston, where each row is a citation from the Public Works Department’s Code Enforcement Division. Dorchester, the city’s largest neighborhood, recorded 13,054 citations of all types this year. The 6,494 for improper residential trash storage make up the largest single category.

The numbers come as city officials continue to emphasize proper waste disposal as a key part of keeping Boston’s neighborhoods clean and limiting rodent activity. In a late August announcement outlining the City’s preparations for September’s busy student move-in period, Mayor Michelle Wu and her administration urged residents to follow trash collection rules and dispose of household waste properly. In the press release, the administration described proper storage and disposal as the “most impactful way to deter rodents.”

“Our crews are prepared for the increased volume of trash, but we need everyone’s help to keep Boston clean,” said Dennis Roache, superintendent of the Waste Reduction Division. He encouraged residents to follow collection schedules and place trash and recycling at the curb.

But the city’s enforcement data suggests the problem extends well beyond seasonal increases in household waste.

How far above average is Dorchester?

To test whether Dorchester’s count is an outlier, improper residential trash storage citations were compared across the six neighborhoods in the cleaned dataset that had all four of the most common violation types. The average across those six is 2,843. Dorchester’s 6,494 is 3,651 citations above that mean, or 128% higher. Compared with the average of the other five alone (2,112), it is 207% higher. Roxbury, at 2,777, is within 3% of the mean, and Mattapan sits 55% below it.

What the data can and can’t say

Dorchester’s 6,494 residential citations represent approximately 22% of Boston’s 30,093. The concentration contrasts with smaller, more affluent neighborhoods like Chestnut Hill, which recorded just 2 citations of any type this year.

The numbers at the very least tell us Boston’s trash storage violations are overwhelmingly residential, and Dorchester accounts for a substantial portion of the city’s enforcement activity.

“By working together and being good neighbors, we can help keep Boston a safe, healthy, and welcoming city for everyone,” said Tania del Rio, inspectional services commissioner, in the same press release.

Limitations

The data doesn’t convey the extremity of each violation, so citations may not be truly comparable. Counts are not adjusted for population or housing units; Dorchester is the city’s largest neighborhood, so a higher total is partly expected. Citations also reflect where inspectors look and respond to complaints, not necessarily where trash problems are worst. The income layer is from 2018 while the citations are from 2026. Finally, thousands of points on one map make dense areas hard to read, and the six-neighborhood comparison omits other neighborhoods.

Data sources

  • Public Works Violations, Analyze Boston (City of Boston). Citation-level records with latitude and longitude from the Code Enforcement Division, updated routinely.

  • Map: commercial vs. residential violations and companion map: Dorchester vs. Chestnut Hill (Tableau Public).

Methods. I filtered the Public Works Violations data to 2026 and cleaned it, correcting ZIP codes to assign each citation to a neighborhood (Violation_City). In Tableau, I plotted unaggregated latitude and longitude, colored points by violation description, set citation counts as tooltips, and added a description filter and a household income background layer. For the chart, I pivoted citations by neighborhood and violation type in Excel, took the mean of improper residential trash storage across the six neighborhoods in that pivot (Back Bay/Beacon Hill, Brighton, Dorchester, Mattapan, Roxbury, South Boston), and calculated each neighborhood’s difference from that mean as a percent. Citywide residential and commercial totals come from a separate pivot of violation types. Neighborhood totals of 13,054 and similar figures count all violation types, whereas 33,225 counts only residential and commercial improper trash storage.
